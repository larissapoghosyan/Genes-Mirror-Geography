Does DNA encode more than biology?

Put two human genomes side by side, and more than 99% of their letters match. What remains seems almost too small to notice. Yet across millions of letters it stores a staggering archive of information. These quiet variations tell us not only how our species evolved, but about the very world in which that evolution unfolded.

02 · FROM LETTERS TO NUMBERS

Turning biology into data

Two chromosomes. One simple count.

Because human genomes are overwhelmingly alike, this analysis focuses on SNPs—precise genomic positions where our DNA letters vary.

Zoom into one of these sites, and we compare the two chromosome copies inherited from our parents. Here, the participant inherited an A from one parent and a G from the other. (Down at the molecular level, the complementary strands pair A with T, and G with C.)

To turn this physical biology into computable data, we simply count a target letter. Tracking copies of G, this AG genotype yields exactly 1. That single integer then slots into our matrix, filling one cell in this individual's row under the SNP's column.

04 · CLEANING THE TABLE

Filtering 276,725 markers
down to 46,213.

Before searching for geographic patterns, we rigorously clean the matrix. We drop sites with missing measurements and filter out rare variants shared by too few people.

Crucially, DNA is inherited in connected chunks. If we kept every neighboring site, those linked blocks would mathematically echo the same ancestral signal over and over. To prevent this distortion, we thin out adjacent markers that merely repeat one another.

Technical note: excludes sex chromosomes and high-LD inversions. Requires 100% call completeness and minor-allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 5% in our 407-person core QC panel. Applies sliding-window LD pruning (50-marker windows, step 5, r² > 0.20).

05 · STANDARDIZATION

Leveling the playing field

Not every genetic difference carries the same weight. Sharing a less common variant with someone can be a stronger clue about shared ancestry. Sharing a common variant carried by half the cohort tells us very little on its own.

If we fed raw counts into PCA, common variants would usually have greater numerical spread and could drown out less common ones. We correct this by comparing each count with the frequency in a fixed European reference panel, then scaling each column so less common variants can be heard.

Technical note: Patterson scaling (based on Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium). For each SNP, p is the allele frequency in a fixed 428-person reference panel. We subtract 2p from each genotype count and divide by 2p(1−p), its expected standard deviation.

06 · DIMENSIONALITY REDUCTION

Finding the best angle to look at the data

You cannot visualize a 46,213-dimensional spreadsheet. But you can spin it until you find the best angle to look at it.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) rotates the data to find the direction that captures the widest spread of the group (PC1). Then it finds the best perpendicular direction (PC2), giving the cloud a flat, 2D shape—like a complex 3D object casting a 2D shadow on a wall.

07 · THE COMPRESSION

46,213 columns collapse into a 2D dot.

Now we unleash that exact algorithm on the real genetic matrix. For every person, the computer compresses their 46,213 standardized DNA measurements into just two master scores: a horizontal position (PC1) and a vertical position (PC2).

About 46,000 biological measurements collapse into a single dot on a blank grid. And here is the crucial detail: zero geographic coordinates entered this calculation.

Technical note: principal directions are learned via singular value decomposition (SVD) on the 428-person reference panel. Each person’s normalized row of 46,213 calls is then multiplied by the top two loading vectors to project their (PC1, PC2) coordinates.

08 · THE UNSUPERVISED STRUCTURE

A continent in the noise
0.96% of variance.

Together, our two master axes capture less than one percent (0.96%) of the standardized genetic variance in the reference panel. Most human genetic variation lies within populations rather than between them; these axes isolate only a thin layer of between-region structure.

Yet across 46,213 markers, that fractional percentage carries a powerful spatial echo. As the matrix gives way to a cloud of dots, we color them using the participants’ recorded regional labels. The data settles into its mathematical shape, and a familiar architecture emerges from the noise.

Technical note: PC1 (0.58%) and PC2 (0.38%) explain 0.96% of reference-panel genetic variance. Regional colors are metadata added only for visual interpretation; they played no role in fitting PCA. This is the unrotated distribution before Procrustes alignment. Explained variance is not geographic prediction accuracy.

09 · ORIENTING THE COMPASS

PCA has no North.

The algorithm found a structure that resembles a continent, but it does not know which way is up. PCA’s axis signs are arbitrary: the genetic map has no geographic North, South, East, or West.

To orient it against geography, we apply one global adjustment to the entire cloud: rotate it by −117.2 degrees, shift it, and scale it uniformly. Every single dot receives the same treatment. There is no selective stretching, warping, or country-by-country nudging. The genetic structure simply spins into alignment with the geographic reference.

Technical note: alignment uses a global Procrustes similarity transform (translation, rotation, uniform scaling) fitted on 52 regional population centroids. The fit uses recorded and assigned locality coordinates and allows a global reflection to account for PC sign ambiguity.

10 · THE REVEAL

Genes mirror geography

Without drawing a single coastline, the data forms a map.

Every dot keeps the same position relative to every other dot. As faint color washes and two-letter country codes identify the participants’ recorded regions, the genetic point cloud traces the recognizable shape of Europe.

The math confirms the visual: genetic coordinates and geography correlate at r = 0.861 East–West and r = 0.881 North–South. This is a pattern consistent with isolation by distance. Over generations, people often lived, moved, and formed families locally, linking genetic similarity to the landscape.

Technical note: color-wash shapes are smoothed from genetic PCA positions; their colors and the country codes come from regional metadata. PCA receives no map target, though the later alignment uses geography. Correlations describe the aligned cohort, not held-out prediction. No coastline is drawn over the genetic points. The small Europe inset is a separate regional color key.

Where the mirror bends

1. Reference sensitivity

Toggle between our quota-balanced 428-person reference and an equal-weight fit on all 996 people. Spain and Italy together make up 43% of the cohort. Watch how that sampling imbalance shifts the axes: the East–West correlation falls from 0.861 to 0.812.

2. Descriptive versus predictive

A clear geographic pattern is not a precise genetic GPS. In earlier 634-person experiments using 50 PCs, out-of-sample models still had mean locality errors of about 425 km (neural) and 458 km (linear). PCA reveals structure; it does not guarantee exact individual prediction.
